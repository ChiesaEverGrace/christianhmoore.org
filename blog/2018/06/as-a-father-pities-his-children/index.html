Being in The Way

As a Father Pities His Children

15 June 2018 · 2018 archive

"As a father pities his children, so the LORD pities those who fear Him. For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust." — Psalm 103:13–14 NKJV

On Father's Day I want to start with the Father.

Watch a summer action movie. Everybody is one-dimensional. Hero. Villain. Warrior. No depth. And because there's no depth, you never really connect with any of them.

That's how a lot of people see God. A force. A kindly grandfather. A stern judge. A buddy. The cause of everything. Flat.

Psalm 103 shows us a Father who is none of those cartoons. He's deep. He's knowable. And He's close.

Compassionate anger

"The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in mercy. He will not always strive with us, nor will He keep His anger forever. He has not dealt with us according to our sins" (103:8–10).

God does get angry. This isn't a God who shrugs at everything.

But His anger is not payback. Not you hurt me, so I'll hurt you. Payback anger in a family never works. It poisons everything.

His anger is slow. Under control. And it's driven by compassion. The compassion is permanent; the anger is temporary.

The more a father loves his son, the more he hates what is destroying his son. The drinking. The lying. The betrayal.

If God never got angry about what we do to ourselves, He wouldn't be good. The opposite of love isn't anger. It's indifference.

Absolute safety

"As a father pities his children…"

That word for compassion is strong. It means to be moved deep in your gut. And it's connected to the word for a mother's womb.

"Can a woman forget her nursing child, and not have compassion on the son of her womb? Surely they may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isaiah 49:15).

The same word shows up in 1 Kings 3. Two women come before Solomon, both claiming the same baby. Solomon calls for a sword: cut the child in two. And the real mother, her heart yearning for her son, cries out, Give her the child. Don't kill him. She'd rather lose him than see him die.

That's the love God says He has for us.

And why? "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust."

We think God loves us more when we're doing well. But a parent's heart goes out most when the child is falling apart. When we're dust, flawed, a mess, that's when His compassion moves.

We are safe in that love. Nothing can separate us. You won't find that kind of safety in anyone on earth. Everyone here is broken to some degree.

Our ultimate home

"As for man, his days are like grass… the wind passes over it, and it is gone, and its place remembers it no more" (103:15–16).

Its place remembers it no more. Those are haunting words.

Have you ever gone back to where you grew up and nobody knows you? The friends are gone. The family has moved. You don't fit anywhere.

Why do so many people spend so much to go back to the place they're from? Why do adopted children long to find their birth parents?

Because we fit into a lot of places. School. Work. Friends. But home is the place that fits you.

Every time Jesus spoke to God, He called Him Father. Except once. On the cross. "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?"

In that moment He was cut off from His home. Like that mother before Solomon, He took the place of the guilty so the child could live.

Jesus lost His home so we never would.

Then comes verse 17. "But the mercy of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting on those who fear Him, and His righteousness to children's children."

No place remembers us? From everlasting to everlasting, He does.

"God sets the solitary in families" (Psalm 68:6). "In My Father's house are many mansions… I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2).

His love is the home.

For us as fathers

"Fathers, do not provoke your children to wrath" (Ephesians 6:4).

Anger that isn't governed by compassion eats everything. It burns through every other emotion in us. Then it burns through our kids until there's nothing left in them. That's an exasperated child.

Some of us under-discipline. We want our kids' approval. We need them to need us.

Some of us over-discipline. We can't stand for them to fail, because their failure humiliates us.

Both come from the same place. Our identity as fathers isn't resting in our Father.

When I know His compassionate anger, His absolute safety and His everlasting home, I'm free. I don't need my kids to make me significant. I can love them the way I've been loved.

Happy Father's Day.
