Being in The Way

Three Kinds of Blindness

25 March 2023 · 2023 archive

"Then he said, 'Lord, I believe!' And he worshiped Him." — John 9:38 NKJV

In John 9 Jesus passes a man who was born blind. And everybody around him is blind too, in a different way.

1. Blind to the meaning of suffering

The disciples ask, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (9:2).

Why always shows up with suffering. And we usually assume the answer. Hard life? You must have done something.

There's pride in that. If suffering is the sufferer's fault, then it can't happen to me, because I'm doing everything right. Job's friends thought that way.

Jesus says, "Neither" (9:3).

When a tower fell at Siloam and killed eighteen people, Jesus asked, "Do you think they were worse sinners than all others?" No. "But unless you repent you will all likewise perish" (Luke 13:4–5).

We live in a broken world. The whole human race suffers because of sin. But Jesus pushes back hard on the idea that your suffering is payback for your sin. He brought a mercy that triumphs over judgment.

And in this man's case, He says there's something higher going on. "That the works of God should be revealed in him."

2. Blind to our own condition

The Pharisees spend most of the chapter interrogating the man, then his parents, then the man again.

They can't see who Jesus is. And they can't see themselves.

There's a kind of sight that goes beyond knowing facts. It's realization. That moment when something you knew suddenly becomes real. Oh. I didn't see it.

Spiritual blindness is missing two realities. The reality of sin and the reality of grace.

Plenty of people will say, "Sure, I'm a sinner." But it isn't real until the Holy Spirit shows you. When He does, you see how deep the problem goes, and how much deeper grace goes.

Paul prayed for this. "The eyes of your understanding being enlightened" (Ephesians 1:18).

3. Blind, and then seeing

The man born blind ends up thrown out of the synagogue.

And Jesus goes and finds him. "Do you believe in the Son of God?" "Who is He, Lord, that I may believe in Him?" "You have both seen Him and it is He who is talking with you." "Lord, I believe!" And he worshiped Him (9:35–38).

The disciples and the Pharisees were stuck arguing about right and wrong. Who sinned? Is Jesus a sinner? The man wasn't at that tree anymore. He was looking at the One who healed him.

What we worship, we are blind to

"For judgment I have come into this world, that those who do not see may see, and that those who see may be made blind" (9:39).

Jesus hints that brilliant, successful people are at a disadvantage. Not because success is wrong. Because whatever we worship, we can't see clearly.

If my work is the measure of my worth, I can't be honest about how I'm doing. I see what I want to see. Or I'm too afraid to look.

But when we worship Him, we see clearly. His love becomes the measure of our worth.

The ones whose eyes get opened aren't the good ones. They're the ones who admit they aren't good, and lean on Jesus.

Sometimes it takes trouble that shakes our world before we can see Him.

The deepest blindness is being blind to your own blindness.

The poet Luci Shaw imagines Mary holding her newborn and realizing that for her own darkness to be healed, she would have to see her Son torn.

That's the cure. We see when we see Him wounded for us.

Do you believe in the Son of God?
